# Human-writing editing guide

Use this guide as a final editing lens for brand copy. It adapts observations from Wikipedia's [Signs of AI writing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing), accessed August 29, 2026. That page is a descriptive field guide, not a universal rulebook, and many of its examples are specific to Wikipedia. A single pattern does not prove AI authorship. The practical lesson for copywriting is to fix the underlying weakness—vagueness, inflation, repetition, false authority, or mechanical structure—not merely swap flagged words.

## What human copy should do

Human-sounding copy feels chosen. Its details, rhythm, emphasis, and omissions fit this brand, this audience, and this moment. It does not sound like competent language that could belong to any company.

Aim for:

- Specific observations instead of broad praise.
- Proportionate claims instead of manufactured importance.
- Direct relationships instead of abstract associations.
- Natural variation instead of repeated rhetorical templates.
- Useful structure instead of visible answer-generation scaffolding.
- Verified facts instead of plausible filler.

Do not add typos, slang, fragments, or eccentric punctuation merely to appear human. Do not optimize for an AI detector. Edit for clarity, specificity, voice, and truth.

## Content checks

### Replace inflated significance with a consequence

AI-like prose often declares that a minor fact marks a turning point, reflects a wider movement, leaves a lasting legacy, or proves the subject's importance. Keep such interpretation only when the evidence supports it and the audience needs it.

Ask: *What changed for the customer, reader, or business?* State that plainly.

- Weak: "This launch marks a pivotal shift in the evolving wellness landscape."
- Better: "The new plan gives members two coaching calls a month instead of one."

### Remove superficial analysis

Watch for an accurate statement followed by an unsupported participial tail that tells the reader what it supposedly demonstrates: "showcasing...", "highlighting...", "underscoring...", "reflecting...", or "ensuring...". Delete the tail or replace it with a sourced, concrete consequence.

### Keep promotion earned

Generic praise weakens persuasive copy. Words such as "groundbreaking," "renowned," "vibrant," "world-class," "seamless," or "exceptional" need evidence or a distinctly intentional brand reason. Show the proof, mechanism, sensory detail, or customer outcome instead.

### Name the source and scope the claim

Avoid vague authorities such as "experts say," "industry reports show," "critics agree," or "customers love" unless the source and evidence are supplied. Do not turn one comment into a consensus or imply more examples than the record contains.

### Do not fill evidence gaps with likelihood

When information is missing, omit the claim, label the gap, or ask for the fact. Do not write speculative bridges such as "likely," "generally," or "based on available information" to keep a paragraph looking complete.

### Avoid the canned ending

Do not automatically close with a "challenges and opportunities," "future outlook," or optimistic resilience paragraph. End where the deliverable's job is complete: the next step, the offer, the decision, the image, or the call to action.

## Language checks

### Watch clusters, not isolated words

AI output often bunches together polished abstractions. Review concentrations of terms such as "pivotal," "crucial," "robust," "delve," "landscape," "foster," "showcase," "underscore," "tapestry," "testament," "enhance," and "align." A word may be right; several in a short passage usually mean the copy needs more concrete language.

### Use simple verbs when they are accurate

Prefer "is," "has," "does," "uses," or a precise action verb when ornate substitutes add no meaning. Phrases such as "serves as," "stands as," "functions as," "boasts," or "offers" can make ordinary facts sound staged.

### State the actual relationship

Replace vague constructions such as "associated with" or "in connection with" with the real link: made by, used for, funded by, hired by, caused by, partnered with, sold through, or mentioned in.

### Break repeated contrast templates

Use "not only X, but Y," "not X—Y," and "rather than" only when the contrast is real and useful. Repeated negative parallelism sounds argumentative and prepackaged. State the affirmative point directly when the rejected alternative was never in question.

### Resist automatic groups of three

Three adjectives, three fragments, and three benefit bullets can create false completeness. Use the number of items the idea requires. Vary list lengths naturally, and remove items added only to complete a pattern.

### Let sentence rhythm follow meaning

Avoid a procession of similarly sized sentences, repeated opening adverbs, and identical paragraph shapes. Mix short and long sentences where emphasis and comprehension call for it. Contractions are welcome when they match `SOUL.md` and the channel.

## Structure and formatting checks

- Do not restate the requested title as a redundant first heading.
- Use sentence case for headings unless the brand or channel specifies otherwise.
- Do not create headings that contain only more headings.
- Prefer prose when a short passage reads more naturally than a list.
- Avoid mechanical bullet patterns where every item starts with a bold label and colon.
- Use bold, italics, emojis, tables, and thematic dividers only when they help the channel and match the brand.
- Treat the em dash as ordinary punctuation, not a dramatic reflex. Where dashes pile up, test a period, comma, colon, or parenthesis.
- Do not add "Key takeaways," "Why it matters," or similar wrappers unless they genuinely help the reader or the channel requires them.

## Remove assistant residue

Deliver copy, not the conversation that produced it. Strip:

- "Here is...", "Of course", "I hope this helps", and offers to provide more.
- Notes about training data, search results, knowledge limits, or the writing process.
- Instructions to the user that accidentally became audience-facing copy.
- Placeholder text, dummy links, bracketed variables, fake dates, and template prompts unless the deliverable intentionally needs merge fields.
- Internal citation tokens, tool markup, code fences, or formatting syntax that the destination cannot render.

## Truth and source check

AI-sounding style is often a symptom of a deeper reliability problem. Do not treat a surface rewrite as enough.

- Verify names, dates, links, quotations, statistics, testimonials, awards, and attributions against supplied sources.
- Never fabricate citations, URLs, customer language, or product capabilities.
- Distinguish the brand's opinion from a factual claim.
- Keep claims within the evidence's actual scope.
- If a required fact cannot be verified, flag it outside the final copy or mark the field clearly for completion.

## Quick read-aloud pass

Read the draft as the intended speaker. Revise any sentence that:

1. Could appear unchanged on a competitor's site.
2. Sounds more impressed than informed.
3. Explains an implication the reader can already see.
4. Uses symmetry where the thought is not symmetrical.
5. Exists to make the draft look comprehensive.
6. Claims authority without naming evidence.
7. Sounds like a helpful assistant instead of the brand.

Stop when the copy is specific, credible, on-voice, and suited to the channel. Do not sand away every unusual phrase; a real voice needs texture.
