# Email Frameworks

Use this guide to choose a structure for the email's job. A framework organizes the argument; it does not replace audience knowledge, a relevant topic, specific proof, or a clear call to action.

## Quick selection guide

| Framework | Choose it when the email should… | Best fit | Avoid when… |
|---|---|---|---|
| **AIDA** | Move the reader from initial attention to action | Announcements, promotions, launches, invitations, and persuasive nurture emails | The reader already knows the offer and only needs a quick answer or reminder |
| **BAB** | Help the reader picture a better future | Benefit-led offers, transformations, case studies, and unfamiliar solutions | The desired outcome is vague, unrealistic, or unsupported |
| **PAS** | Make a known problem feel important to solve now | Pain-aware audiences, urgent problems, first-touch outreach, and problem-led offers | The problem is speculative or agitation would feel manipulative |
| **QVC** | Start or restart a low-pressure conversation | Follow-ups, reactivation, stalled decisions, warm leads, and simple outreach | There is no relevant question, prior value, or new reason to contact the reader |

If more than one framework fits, choose based on the email's dominant job: **persuade broadly → AIDA; sell the outcome → BAB; prioritize a problem → PAS; earn a reply → QVC.**

## AIDA — Attention, Interest, Desire, Action

**How it works**

1. **Attention:** Open with a relevant hook, observation, benefit, or timely trigger.
2. **Interest:** Connect the hook to something the reader cares about.
3. **Desire:** Make the value concrete through benefits, proof, or a believable outcome.
4. **Action:** Ask for one clear, low-friction next step.

**Why it works:** It follows the reader's progression from noticing the message to wanting the outcome and acting on it.

**Agent guidance:** Lead with the reader, not the sender. Keep each stage proportional to the reader's awareness; a familiar offer may need only a sentence per stage. Do not mistake a clever subject line for relevant attention or introduce the CTA before earning interest.

**Skeleton:** `Relevant hook → why it matters → desirable outcome + proof → one CTA`

[Source: JB Sales](https://learn.jbarrows.com/pages/quality-outreach-aida-framework)

## BAB — Before, After, Bridge

**How it works**

1. **Before:** Describe the reader's current problem, limitation, or unmet need.
2. **After:** Show a specific, believable future in which that issue is resolved.
3. **Bridge:** Explain how the offer moves the reader from the current state to the desired one.
4. **CTA:** End with one next step; the CTA is necessary even though it is not part of the acronym.

**Why it works:** Contrast makes the benefit easy to visualize, while the bridge gives the reader a credible path to it.

**Agent guidance:** Make the reader—not the product—the main character. Focus the “After” on outcomes rather than features, and support the bridge with a relevant mechanism, example, or proof point. Avoid inflated before-and-after claims.

**Skeleton:** `Current state → improved state → offer as the credible path → one CTA`

[Source: GMass](https://www.gmass.co/blog/before-after-bridge-copywriting/)

## PAS — Problem, Agitate, Solve

**How it works**

1. **Problem:** Name one real, relevant problem clearly.
2. **Agitate:** Make the consequence of leaving it unresolved concrete—such as lost time, added cost, frustration, or risk.
3. **Solve:** Present the offer as a practical way to address the problem, then give one CTA.

**Why it works:** It turns a recognized pain point into a priority before introducing the solution.

**Agent guidance:** Agitate with evidence and empathy, not fear. Use only consequences that are plausible for this reader. Keep the solution focused on relief or improvement rather than a feature list. If the problem is not verified or strongly supported by context, choose another framework.

**Skeleton:** `Specific problem → concrete cost of inaction → solution benefit → one CTA`

[Source: AiSDR](https://aisdr.com/blog/email-framework-pas/)

## QVC — Question, Value, Closing

*The source commonly names the final step “Close”; “Closing” has the same function.*

**How it works**

1. **Question:** Open with a relevant, easy-to-answer question that creates a natural reason to reply.
2. **Value:** Briefly state the useful outcome, insight, reminder, or resource being offered.
3. **Closing:** Ask for one specific, low-effort next step.

**Why it works:** It lowers pressure, centers the exchange on the reader, and makes responding easy.

**Agent guidance:** Use QVC when the goal is conversation rather than a full sales argument. For reactivation, anchor the question to a genuine new trigger, prior interest, or value the reader already cared about. Avoid empty openers such as “just checking in.”

**Skeleton:** `Relevant question → concise value → easy reply or next step`

[Source: Unify](https://www.unifygtm.com/explore/cold-email-frameworks-b2b-saas?08608ed1_page=2&639a0fac_page=2&6deaca0b_page=3)

## Rules that apply to every framework

- Choose the framework **after** identifying the audience, topic, desired outcome, and CTA.
- Build the email around one main idea and one primary action.
- Use specific, verifiable details; never invent pain, urgency, proof, or personalization.
- Match the reader's awareness. Do not re-explain what they already know.
- Treat the framework as flexible structure, not visible labels or a fill-in-the-blank script.
- Prefer the shortest version that makes the reasoning complete.
